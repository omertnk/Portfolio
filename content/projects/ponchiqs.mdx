## Overview

Ponchiqs is a free-to-play multiplayer third-person shooter built in Unreal Engine 5 and released in Early Access on the Epic Games Store by Ponchiqs Studio. It mixes a party-style shooter with customizable "psycho powers": matches are fast and vertical, with players hooking onto terrain, gliding with umbrellas, skating across the map and fighting inside a shrinking zone.

I joined the studio as a Gameplay Programmer and owned most of the movement and traversal layer. Every mechanic below is an ability in the Gameplay Ability System and works in multiplayer. Movement-critical abilities such as the hook and jumps are client-predicted so they respond on the same frame; systems like the zone and level volumes stay purely server-authoritative.

## What I built

- **Hook** — grapple onto surfaces and pull the character through the air; replicated rope state and predicted movement so it feels instant on the client
- **Umbrella glide** — deploy an umbrella mid-air to slow the fall and steer; used both for traversal and for escaping fights
- **Skates** — ground traversal mode with its own acceleration and turning model for crossing the map quickly
- **Zone system** — Fortnite/PUBG-style shrinking play area with phase timers, damage outside the circle and server-driven center selection
- **Trampoline & super jump** — level-placed launch pads and an ability-driven high jump for vertical movement
- **Speed boost & mud areas** — level volumes that apply movement modifiers to any player inside; boost accelerates, mud slows
- **Ability framework** — all of the above built on the Gameplay Ability System so designers could tune cooldowns, costs and effects without code changes

## Systems

### Multiplayer movement on the Ability System

Every traversal mechanic is a `GameplayAbility` that drives the character's movement component. The server owns the truth. For abilities where input latency would be felt — hook, super jump, trampoline — the local client predicts activation and movement, and the server validates and corrects if needed. Abilities where a few milliseconds do not matter run server-authoritative only, which keeps them simpler and cheaper.

Ability state (active hook, gliding, skate mode) replicates through Gameplay Tags, which also define what can combine with what. Gliding with the umbrella does not block the hook: the player can fire the hook mid-glide, and the hook takes over movement when it attaches.

### Hook

The hook is the most complex ability: a replicated projectile, an attach phase, and a pull phase that overrides the movement mode. The main challenge was keeping the rope visually stable for other clients while the local player's prediction ran ahead of the server.

<Figure src="/projects/ponchiqs/hook.gif" caption="Hook: fire → attach → pull, with the rope synced for spectators." />

### Zone

The zone is a server-only system: it picks the next circle, broadcasts phase timers to clients, and applies a damage Gameplay Effect to anyone outside. Clients only render the circle and the countdown, so the system stays cheap over the network.

<Figure src="/projects/ponchiqs/zone.png" caption="Zone phases — shrink timing and damage per phase are data-driven." />

### Level volumes

Speed boost and mud areas are placed by level designers as volumes. On overlap the volume applies a Gameplay Effect with a movement-speed modifier; on exit it removes it. Because the effect is data, designers could add new area types (e.g. a jump-boost pad) without touching code.

## Challenges & learnings

This project was where I learned multiplayer for real. Getting a mechanic to work locally is a fraction of the job; making it feel identical for the owning client, other clients and the server is the rest. The biggest lessons were around prediction — which abilities to predict, how to reconcile when the server disagrees, and how to keep cosmetic state (rope, umbrella, skate effects) in sync without spending bandwidth on it.

Building everything on the Ability System paid off: once the framework was in place, a new mechanic was mostly configuration, and designers could balance it themselves.
